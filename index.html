Kyle Flores — kyflores@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Easier access to abortion increases abortion rate.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Data Transformation: 1.5 Deceptiveness
    Creating the abortion access score consisted of finding the mean of 3 categories: % of counties without a known clinic, % of residents who traveled out of state for the abortion, and % of women living in a county without a clinic. This decision helps make the visualization persuasive by simplifying complex, multidimensional data into a single interpretable score that represents “access”. Doing this worked well because it represented the availability and travel burden of abortion, while also showing a clear separation between different states to indicate that states are indeed different from each other in terms of abortion. A limitation of this, however, is that it did not capture every single feature associated with “access” within the dataset while also giving each feature a similar weight, which oversimplified some complexities within the data. Other alternatives included a PCA analysis or generating several graphs for different features, but this method was decided upon because it was clearer and more interpretable upon simple first glances while the other methods would need more time to understand the message of the image.
  • State Filtering: 2 Deceptiveness
    Choosing only the states with the top 7 highest and bottom 7 lowest “Abortion Access Difficulties” helped in making the visualization more persuasive because it showed the patterns among the extreme ends of the difficulty spectrum. This contrast made the differences in access easier for viewers to grasp quickly. It also reduced the total visual clutter by reducing the total amount of bars, allowing for a cleaner and more focused presentation of the main message. While this approach sacrifices some nuance by omitting middle-range states and preventing the reader from viewing the pattern within the totality of the country, it effectively emphasized the indication of different levels of abortion access in the U.S. Other options, such as including all states, were considered, but they cluttered the plot and made the visual harder to interpret at a glance. Grouping the states into regions was also considered, but oversimplified the patterns within the data and prevented a clear message from being presented.
  • Colored Visual Encoding: 1.5 Deceptiveness
    Adding in the line for the nationwide average difficulty as well as text to mark the areas of low difficulty and high difficulty in corresponding text colors help make the visualization more persuasive by explicitly pointing out that the presented information represents the extreme ends of the abortion access spectrum. These annotations guide the viewer’s attention and clearly convey that message without requiring deep analysis of the data. Green is used for low difficulty because green is commonly associated with ease and accessibility, which aligns with the concept of having fewer barriers to abortion access. On the other hand, red is used for high difficulty because red is typically linked to warning signs and stop signals, portraying the idea of increased barriers or obstacles towards abortion. While the plot was initially readable without these elements, adding them made the message even clearer. Some issues that this design choice could bring is overstimulation, but this seems like a stretch considering the lack of total ink added to the image. Other options, including a legend or additional explanatory notes, were considered but felt redundant in the presence of a subtitle or distracting given that the graph was already decently filled with information.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Filtering Out Outliers: -1.5 Deceptiveness
    Filtering out the two states with the highest number of clinics due to them being outliers helped make the visualization more persuasive by removing two bars that would be in support of the proposition. Removing them gave off a stronger impression that clinic amount does not relate to higher abortion rates, making the graph something that can go against the proposition. However, because the two states were deemed as outliers by IQR outliers detection, this design choice is not completely deceptive. This worked well at contrasting the proposition, but also is a warning to more informed-on-abortion readers that this image could be deceptive since California is the leading state in abortion clinics, but is not present within the graph. Some other alternatives were changing the colors of the bars throughout the entire spectrum to be pretty similar and prevent a clear gradient from being viewable, but this would clearly indicate deception considering the color gradient is present to the side of the graph.
  • Colored and Bolded Text Annotation: 1 Deceptiveness
    Adding in the text that “Abortion Rates Do Not Clearly Reflect Clinic Availability” in red bolded font in a text box excels at getting across the main message of the image. It makes the visualization more persuasive by explaining the main message of the image at a single glance, without the graph needing to be interpreted or studied. This design choice worked well because it quickly directs the viewer's attention and anchors their interpretation of the chart. Alternatives like a separate explanatory paragraph were considered, but a prominent text box within the graph itself proved more effective for immediate clarity and visual impact.
  • Filtering to Top States: 0.5 Deceptiveness
    Only including the top 10, excluding outliers, states in terms of total number of clinics gives a large enough sample size to see a pattern within the data while also showing that very high amounts of abortion clinics do not mean that there are more abortions. Including more than 10 would clutter the graph for no real purpose, since no pattern was visible among the rest of the data.

Final Reflection

The most straightforward part of the design process was filtering out the middle states to only show the extreme ends of the spectrum for whatever independent variable was being explored. Because there were so many states, it was clear that removing many of the states would be crucial to building a persuasive and concise visualization. Doing this made patterns and arguments within the data easier to visualize in support of the proposition, while also being important for conveying the other side of the proposition. On the other hand, some difficulties were encountered when deciding the color code for the abortion rate on either graph. Trying to decide on a color scheme that could be biased to bring deception or finding a color scheme that would give the viewer an inherent idea of the scale without need for explanation was difficult. Since several designs aimed at these purposes seemed ineffective or unnecessary, the final design was created with a color scheme that was unbiased and did not add any deception to the visuals. Some parts of the process that brought surprises were that previous ideas, either for/against the proposition, were not displayed by the data. This led to deeper analysis and more creative ideas to create arguments for the proposition.

From working on this project, my definition of “ethical analysis and visualization” consists of representing the data in a transparent and honest way that informs the viewer without distorting the apparent message. Ethical visualization means making persuasive design choices that clarify instead of manipulating—such as adding annotations or color cues to highlight patterns—while avoiding deceptive tactics like cherry-picking data or using deceptive scales. Persuasive choices that should be accepted are choices that emphasize what the data genuinely shows, while misleading choices are ones that alter interpretation to suggest relationships or conclusions that aren’t inherently supported by the data.
